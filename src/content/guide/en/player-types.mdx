Every player in Ice Flow has a type. It is not a label on top of the attributes - it shapes them,
decides which tactics a player suits and which attributes grow faster in training. This page explains
where the types come from and what each one means.

## Where the types come from

The skater types are based on the player profiles that coaches and scouts use, as described by
Identity Shift Hockey in *The 14 Hockey Player Archetypes* (see [Sources](../sources/)). Ice Flow uses
ten of them - six for forwards and four for defensemen, two of the defensive profiles merged into one.
The names are kept in English in every language, as in the app.

<Callout type="note" title="A starting point, not a formula">
  The article defines what each type of player does on the ice. How strongly that shows in the
  game - in attributes, tactics and training - is Ice Flow's own design, tuned by calibration.
</Callout>

## Forwards

<Rules>
  <Rule icon="sports_hockey" title="Sniper">
    Gets open and shoots first whenever he can. **Strong:** shooting, positioning, puck control.
    **Weaker:** body checking, blocking. More technique than physique. The rarest forward type, as in
    the source.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="sports_hockey" title="Playmaker">
    Creates through passing and keeps the puck. **Strong:** passing, puck control, decisions.
    **Weaker:** stick and body checking. More technique than physique.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="sports_hockey" title="Power forward">
    Drives the net, protects the puck down low and plays physically. **Strong:** body checking,
    shooting, positioning. **Weaker:** coverage, blocking. More physique than technique.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="sports_hockey" title="Two-way">
    Plays a low-risk game at both ends, wins the puck back and keeps it. **Strong:** decisions,
    positioning, puck control. **Weaker:** body checking, blocking.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="sports_hockey" title="Defensive">
    His first job is not to get scored on; he plays it safe and shadows the opponent's best players.
    **Strong:** decisions, coverage, blocking. **Weaker:** passing, skating.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="sports_hockey" title="Grinder">
    Simple, reliable plays and physical pressure on defensemen down low. **Strong:** body checking,
    stick checking, positioning. **Weaker:** coverage, decisions. More physique than technique.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

## Defensemen

<Rules>
  <Rule icon="shield" title="Shutdown">
    Takeaways, hits and puck recoveries; plays deep in his own zone and shuts down top players.
    **Strong:** coverage, blocking, stick checking. **Weaker:** positioning, shooting. More physique
    than technique.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="shield" title="Disruptor">
    Takes more risks to create turnovers - pressure on the puck, closed passing lanes, denied
    entries. **Strong:** stick checking, body checking, skating. **Weaker:** passing, shooting.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="shield" title="Puck-moving">
    Pass first: makes the first breakout pass and starts the attack. **Strong:** decisions, passing,
    skating. **Weaker:** body checking, shooting.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="shield" title="Offensive">
    Shoots, joins the rush and acts as a fourth forward. **Strong:** shooting, puck control, passing.
    **Weaker:** blocking, body checking. More technique than physique.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

## Goalies

Goalie types follow the goalie's own technical attributes.

<Rules>
  <Rule icon="sports_hockey" title="Butterfly">
    **Strong:** positioning, puck tracking, blocker. **Weaker:** glove, skating.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="sports_hockey" title="Hybrid">
    **Strong:** skating, reflexes, rebound control. **Weaker:** decisions, blocker. The most common
    goalie type.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="sports_hockey" title="Athletic">
    **Strong:** reflexes, puck tracking, glove. **Weaker:** blocker, positioning.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

## What the type changes

<Rules>
  <Rule icon="tune" title="Attributes">
    The attributes a type is built on sit above the player's level, the rest below. Two players of
    the same level but different types are good at different things.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="palette" title="Training">
    Attributes below the type's level train faster, those far above it barely move - the fit colours
    in training show this. See [Training](../training/).
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="route" title="Tactics">
    Each tactic suits some types, leaves most as they are and does not suit a few.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

### How the table was made

Every one of the 120 combinations of tactic and type was audited on its own. For each, the audit
asked one question: *can this type of player do the job this system gives him?* The answer needed two
documented pieces - what the system asks of the player, from coaching materials and analyses, and
what defines the type, from the player profiles - and the reasoning that joins them.

<Rules>
  <Rule icon="check_circle" title="Suits">
    The system's main job overlaps with what defines the type, or the type has a specific, documented
    advantage in it.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="radio_button_unchecked" title="Neither">
    The type can do the job like any player, or the evidence was not clear enough. Unclear cases get
    no bonus and no penalty.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="cancel" title="Does not suit">
    There is a concrete conflict between the system and what defines the type - for example a
    dump-and-chase game takes the puck away from a playmaker.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

<Callout type="note" title="Balance rules">
  No tactic is unsuitable for more than three types. Fit is counted against an average player, so a
  typical line is equal in every tactic and no tactic is best on its own - one unsuited player does
  not break a system, but a line built for it gains an edge. A cell only changes with new evidence
  about the player's role in the system.
</Callout>
