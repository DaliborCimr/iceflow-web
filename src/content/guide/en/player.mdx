A player in IceFlow is more than one number. His attributes say what he can do, his experience how
reliably he does it, his form how he has been playing lately, and his traits how he develops over a
career. This page goes through them in that order.

## Attributes and ratings

<Rules>
  <Rule icon="tune" title="Technical attributes">
    Shooting, passing, puck control, decisions, positioning, coverage, stick checking, body
    checking, blocking and skating. They decide how a player handles play with and without the puck.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="fitness_center" title="Physical attributes">
    Speed, strength and stamina. Who gets to the puck first, who wins a battle along the boards and
    how fast a player tires.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="sports_hockey" title="Goalies">
    Goalies have their own technical attributes: reflexes, puck tracking, rebound control, glove and
    blocker.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="star" title="Ratings">
    The app sums the attributes up as ratings for attack (ATT), defence (DEF), physical strength
    (PHY) and goalkeeping (GOA), with experience (EXP) next to them.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

## Player types

Every player has a type, and the type shapes the whole player.

| Position | Types |
|---|---|
| Forwards | Sniper, Playmaker, Power forward, Two-way, Defensive, Grinder |
| Defensemen | Shutdown, Disruptor, Puck-moving, Offensive |
| Goalies | Butterfly, Hybrid, Athletic |

<Rules>
  <Rule icon="category" title="How attributes are spread">
    The attributes a type is built on sit above the player's level, the rest below - a sniper is
    high in shooting, a playmaker in passing and decisions.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="balance" title="Technique and physique">
    A sniper, playmaker or offensive defenseman has more technique and less physique; a power
    forward, grinder or shutdown defenseman the other way round.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="route" title="Tactics and training">
    The type decides which tactics suit the player, and in training the attributes below the type's
    level grow faster.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

The types follow the player profiles coaches use (see [Sources](../sources/)).

## Experience: how reliably a player plays

Experience is one of the things that set IceFlow apart, so it deserves more than a line.

### Every game is a different day

Real players do not play at exactly the same level every night. In IceFlow every player has his
**day** in every match: before the game his skills shift a little up or down for that game only.
Experience decides how big that shift can be.

- A player **with little experience** can have a great night, but also a very poor one - and his
  bad nights go deeper than his good nights go high.
- A **veteran** plays close to his real level almost every game. He rarely surprises, in either
  direction.
- For everyone, most games are close to the usual level; the extremes are rare, but they happen.

<Callout type="note" title="Professionalism decides the direction, not the size">
  A professional player is a little more likely to have a good day than a bad one. How far the day
  can swing is still a matter of experience.
</Callout>

### What else experience does

<Rules>
  <Rule icon="battery_charging_full" title="Saves energy">
    An experienced player spends a little less energy on the same shift than the opponents on the
    ice with him.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="gavel" title="Fewer penalties">
    Experience is part of what keeps a player out of the penalty box, together with his decisions
    and discipline.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="trending_up" title="Fair form">
    Form judges a player against what a player of his experience usually delivers, so a young
    player is not marked down just for being less steady.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="school" title="How it grows">
    Experience grows with every game played, fastest for young players.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

### What it means for you

- For a **playoff run** or a tight game, a line of experienced players is the safer choice: you know
  what you will get.
- A **young, talented** line can win you a game on its own - and lose you one. Give it games where a
  bad night does not cost much, and it gains the experience it needs.
- When you compare two players with similar ratings, look at **EXP**: the more experienced one will
  deliver those ratings more often.

<Callout type="note" title="Where this comes from">
  That young players are less consistent and veterans steadier is a common observation in hockey,
  but there is little published research that measures game-to-game variation by experience. The
  model is therefore a design decision of the game, built and calibrated in its match engine.
</Callout>

## Form

<Rules>
  <Rule icon="trending_up" title="Form">
    A bonus or penalty of up to ±2 on the attributes, from how a player has played over roughly his
    last 10-20 games compared with what was expected of him. It is not a swing from one game to the
    next - that is the day, above.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="arrow_outward" title="The arrow">
    Blue up is the best form, green up-right good, white right average, orange down-right poor and
    red down the worst.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

## Traits

Five traits describe what kind of person and athlete the player is. They change slowly or not at all.

<Rules columns>
  <Rule icon="diamond" title="Talent">
    How fast and how far he develops technically.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="workspace_premium" title="Professionalism">
    How he prepares - it shows in physical training, recovery and how long he keeps his fitness as
    he gets older.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="healing" title="Injury resistance">
    How easily he gets hurt. It drops with age and after serious injuries.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="gavel" title="Discipline">
    How often he takes a penalty and how easily he gets drawn into a fight.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="psychology" title="Mentality">
    How he handles pressure in big, tense moments. It can grow over a career from games played
    under pressure.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="unfold_more" title="Only an estimate">
    The club knows a range: two arrows show the lowest and highest possible level. Professional
    scouting narrows it for your own players over time.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

Traits are game abstractions. From research the game takes only the direction - for example that
personality becomes more stable with age, or that a previous injury raises the risk of another - not
the numbers (see [Sources](../sources/)).

## Energy, fatigue and injuries

<Rules>
  <Rule icon="battery_charging_full" title="Energy">
    Drops during a game and comes back on the bench. A player low on energy plays worse.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="bedtime" title="Fatigue">
    Builds up with games and hard training and caps the energy he starts a game with. It goes down
    with rest - by 5 when the afternoon and the evening begin and by 10 overnight.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="local_hospital" title="Injuries">
    An injured player misses games and training for the blocks the injury lasts; better medical
    staff shorten it. At kickoff the best healthy reserve takes his place.
  </Rule>
  <Rule icon="smart_display" title="Shorter recovery">
    Once per game day each injured player can watch an ad to cut his recovery by one block.
  </Rule>
</Rules>

## Development and age

Players improve through training and games - young ones fastest, and more slowly the closer they get
to their potential. From around thirty the body fades on its own: speed first, stamina from the
mid-thirties. Experience keeps veterans steady until they retire.

This follows the shape of real careers: research on NHL players puts the scoring peak at about 27-28
for forwards and 28-29 for defensemen, with near-peak performance over a wide range of ages (Brander,
Egan and Yeung, 2014; see [Sources](../sources/)).
